Climate Tech Breakthroughs That Are Actually Making a Difference

By Elena Torres | June 13, 2025 | 7 min read

Renewable energy deployment has accelerated dramatically, but the harder challenge of decarbonizing heavy industry remains. (Image: Unsplash)

The climate technology landscape in 2025 looks markedly different from the one that existed just five years ago. Costs for solar and wind power have fallen far faster than even optimistic forecasts predicted, electric vehicle adoption is accelerating across most major markets, and a new generation of technologies — long confined to research papers and demonstration projects — is beginning to reach commercial scale.

Not everything has gone according to plan. Carbon capture projects have been slower and more expensive than hoped. Green hydrogen remains challenging to produce economically at scale. And global emissions, while growing more slowly than they once were, have not yet begun the sustained decline that climate scientists say is necessary to limit warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.

But a realistic assessment of where things stand reveals genuine, significant progress in several key areas.

Battery Storage: The Game Changer

The single most consequential development in clean energy over the past decade has arguably been the dramatic fall in battery costs. The cost of lithium-ion battery packs has dropped by more than 90 percent since 2010, according to BloombergNEF data, and the trajectory continues downward.

This cost reduction has simultaneously driven the electric vehicle revolution and made utility-scale battery storage economically viable. Grid-scale battery installations — which store excess solar and wind energy for use when the sun isn't shining and the wind isn't blowing — have grown from a curiosity to a significant piece of power grid infrastructure in less than a decade.

California now routinely runs on 100 percent renewable energy for extended periods during spring and early summer, with battery storage filling gaps that would previously have required natural gas peaker plants. Texas, despite a grid dominated by fossil fuels, has also seen explosive growth in utility-scale battery deployment, driven purely by economics.

Next-Generation Solar

Standard silicon solar panels are approaching the theoretical efficiency limit for single-junction cells. The frontier has shifted to perovskite solar cells and tandem cell designs that layer different materials to capture a broader spectrum of sunlight.

In April 2025, a research team at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory set a new world record for solar cell efficiency at 47.6 percent using a six-junction concentrator cell — far above the roughly 22–24 percent efficiency of standard commercial silicon panels. While concentrator systems are too expensive for rooftop use, the underlying materials science is informing next-generation designs that could reach commercial markets within five to seven years.

"We're not at the ceiling — we're still climbing," says Dr. Sarah Kurtz, a photovoltaics researcher and former NREL scientist. "The efficiency gains of the next decade could be as dramatic as those of the last."

Direct Air Capture: Early Days, Real Progress

Direct air capture (DAC) — technology that pulls carbon dioxide directly from the atmosphere — has moved from science fiction to operational reality, even if it remains extremely expensive. Climeworks, the Swiss DAC company, operates the world's largest facility in Iceland, where geothermal energy powers fans that draw air across chemical filters that bind CO2. The captured carbon is then injected into basalt rock formations, where it mineralizes within two years.

The cost: roughly $1,000 per metric ton of CO2 removed. For context, a meaningful climate impact would require removing billions of tons annually at a cost of under $100 per ton. The gap is vast, but proponents argue that DAC follows the same learning curve as solar — costs fall dramatically as deployment scales up and manufacturing processes improve.

The U.S. Department of Energy has committed $3.5 billion to DAC hub development, and the first large-scale American DAC facility broke ground in Texas in late 2024. Whether it can hit the cost targets needed to become a significant climate tool remains the central question.

Industrial Decarbonization: The Hard Problem

Electricity generation and passenger vehicles get most of the climate technology attention, but together they represent roughly 40 percent of global emissions. The remaining 60 percent — industrial processes like steel and cement production, aviation, shipping, agriculture, and buildings — is significantly harder to decarbonize.

Steel production alone accounts for about 7 percent of global CO2 emissions. The traditional process uses coal-fired blast furnaces that are integral to the chemistry, not just the energy supply. A handful of companies, including Sweden's HYBRIT consortium, have developed hydrogen-based steel production processes that emit water instead of CO2. The first commercial-scale hydrogen steel plant began operating in Sweden in 2023.

The challenge is scaling these technologies from demonstration projects to the massive industrial infrastructure that produces the materials the global economy runs on — and doing it fast enough to matter for the climate.

The Bottom Line

The trajectory of climate technology is genuinely encouraging in ways that were not obvious a decade ago. Clean electricity is now the cheapest form of new electricity generation in most of the world. Electric vehicles are approaching cost parity with internal combustion vehicles without subsidies in several markets. Battery storage is transforming grid operations.

But the pace of deployment needs to accelerate dramatically to meet climate targets, and the hard-to-decarbonize sectors of the economy have barely begun their transition. The technologies exist or are emerging. The question is whether the investment, policy support, and sheer organizational will exist to deploy them at the necessary scale and speed.
