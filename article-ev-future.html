The Future of Electric Vehicles: What to Expect by 2030

By Marcus Webb | June 11, 2025 | 6 min read

The electric vehicle market is accelerating rapidly, with global sales topping 20 million units in 2024. (Image: Unsplash)

In 2015, electric vehicles represented less than one percent of global new car sales. By 2024, that figure had risen to roughly 18 percent — more than 20 million units sold in a single year, according to the International Energy Agency. The transition to electric transportation, long anticipated but frequently delayed, is now proceeding faster than the most optimistic scenarios from just a few years ago.

What happens between now and 2030 will depend on a constellation of factors: battery technology improvements, charging infrastructure deployment, government policy, and the pricing strategies of automakers operating on razor-thin margins in an intensely competitive market. Here is a realistic look at the forces shaping the next five years.

Battery Technology: Continued Cost Decline

The central economic reality of electric vehicles is the battery, which typically represents 30 to 40 percent of the vehicle's total cost. As battery pack costs fall, EVs become more affordable. The trajectory has been steep: costs have fallen from roughly $1,200 per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to around $110 per kilowatt-hour in 2024, according to BloombergNEF.

The industry widely believes $100 per kWh is the threshold at which electric vehicles reach upfront cost parity with equivalent internal combustion vehicles without subsidies. Most analysts expect that threshold to be crossed broadly by 2026 to 2028, depending on the vehicle segment.

Beyond lithium-ion chemistry improvements, solid-state batteries are advancing toward commercialization. Solid-state batteries replace the liquid electrolyte in conventional lithium-ion cells with a solid material, enabling higher energy density (longer range in a smaller, lighter pack), faster charging, and improved safety. Toyota, Samsung SDI, and several well-funded startups have announced target commercial production dates between 2027 and 2030.

Charging Infrastructure: Closing the Gap

Range anxiety — the fear of running out of charge before reaching a charger — has been one of the most persistent barriers to EV adoption, particularly for buyers without access to home charging. Significant investment over the past three years has meaningfully changed the landscape.

The United States now has over 60,000 public charging stations, up from about 28,000 in 2020. The federally-funded National Electric Vehicle Infrastructure program, allocating $5 billion over five years, is specifically targeting the gap in rural and highway charging coverage. Tesla's decision to open its Supercharger network to other manufacturers — following an agreement that has since been adopted by essentially every major automaker — was a pivotal moment that instantly expanded the effective charging network for all EV owners.

Fast charging technology has also improved dramatically. Current DC fast chargers can add 150 to 200 miles of range in 20 to 30 minutes. Next-generation 350 kW chargers, now appearing at highway locations, can add 100 miles of range in under 10 minutes for vehicles with compatible charging hardware.

The Model Proliferation

In 2020, the EV market was dominated by a handful of models, primarily from Tesla. By 2025, virtually every major automaker offers multiple electric models across multiple segments. The competitive implications are significant: more choice means better matching of vehicle attributes to consumer needs, and intensifying competition is accelerating the pace of feature and price improvement.

The entry-level segment — vehicles priced below $30,000 — remains underdeveloped in the United States, though several models in this range are selling in high volumes in China and Europe. Closing this gap is critical for broad-market adoption, as median household incomes in most countries make sub-$30,000 vehicles the primary purchase option for the majority of consumers.

"The EV transition was initially a luxury technology story. The next chapter is a mass-market story, and that requires a fundamentally different approach to cost, range, and simplicity," says Jessica Caldwell, executive director of insights at Edmunds. "The industry knows this — the question is execution."

The Grid Question

A common objection to EVs is that they are only as clean as the electricity grid that charges them. This is true, and in regions that rely heavily on coal-fired power generation, the lifecycle emissions advantage of an EV over a modern gasoline vehicle is modest. But the grid is getting cleaner rapidly, and an EV purchased today will be charged on an increasingly clean grid over its 10-to-15-year lifetime.

Even on the current U.S. grid — which still includes significant fossil fuel generation — the average EV produces about 40 percent fewer lifecycle carbon emissions than the average new gasoline vehicle. In states with clean grids, like California, Washington, and New York, that figure approaches 80 percent.

What to Expect by 2030

By 2030, most analysts project that EVs will represent 30 to 45 percent of new vehicle sales globally — concentrated in China, Europe, and North America. The entry-level price gap between EVs and equivalent gasoline vehicles will have largely closed. Charging networks will be dense enough in most urban and suburban areas that range anxiety will be a minor concern for most buyers.

Significant challenges remain: used-EV market development, battery recycling and second-life applications, and the needs of buyers without access to home charging — primarily urban renters. These are solvable problems, but they require intentional policy and infrastructure investment.

The internal combustion engine has not been defeated yet, and global gasoline demand will likely not peak until the early 2030s. But the direction of travel is clear. The electric vehicle transition is real, it is accelerating, and the 2020s will likely be remembered as the decade it became irreversible.
