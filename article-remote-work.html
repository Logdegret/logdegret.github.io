Remote Work in 2025: Why Companies Are Sticking With Hybrid

By Linda Zhao | June 14, 2025 | 5 min read

The home office has become a permanent fixture for millions of knowledge workers. (Image: Unsplash)

In early 2022, many executives confidently predicted that by 2025, offices would be full again. The post-pandemic "return to office" mandate was framed as a matter of when, not if. Three years later, the data tells a different story.

According to a comprehensive survey of 1,200 large employers conducted by the Future Forum consortium in early 2025, 71 percent of knowledge-work companies now operate under a formal hybrid policy — meaning employees come into the office two or three days per week and work remotely the rest of the time. Only 12 percent had returned to five-day in-office requirements, and that number has been falling, not rising, for the past two years.

What Happened to the Return-to-Office Mandate?

Several high-profile companies — Amazon, Goldman Sachs, and JPMorgan Chase among them — did issue firm five-day return-to-office mandates. What followed, at many of those organizations, was a well-documented wave of voluntary attrition, particularly among senior and highly skilled employees who had the most options.

A Stanford economist's ongoing research into work-from-home patterns found that employees who were required to return full-time were 35 percent more likely to voluntarily leave within six months compared to those at organizations that maintained hybrid arrangements. In a tight labor market for specialized skills, that turnover cost — averaging $21,000 per employee when including recruiting and onboarding expenses — has been a powerful moderating force.

"The return-to-office battle is effectively over, and hybrid won," says Dr. Nicholas Bloom, a Stanford economist who has studied remote work for over a decade. "The companies that tried to force everyone back five days a week mostly just succeeded in losing their best people."

Productivity: The Evidence

The productivity question has been the most contested aspect of the remote work debate. Early pandemic-era studies showing productivity gains from full remote work have been complicated by subsequent research showing that the gains were uneven — strongest for experienced individual contributors, weaker for newer employees and roles requiring high levels of collaboration and mentorship.

The emerging consensus among researchers is that hybrid arrangements capture most of the benefits of both modes: remote days allow for focused, deep work free from office interruptions, while in-office days facilitate the spontaneous collaboration, mentorship, and relationship-building that remote environments handle poorly.

Microsoft's Work Trend Index 2025 found that employees in structured hybrid arrangements reported the highest scores for both productivity and sense of belonging — outperforming both fully remote and fully in-office employees on both dimensions.

The Geographic Redistribution of Talent

One underappreciated consequence of the hybrid shift is geographic. When the requirement to commute to a city center every day was lifted, many workers relocated — not necessarily far, but enough to change their calculus. The 2020–2023 period saw significant population flows out of expensive coastal cities, with mid-size metros like Austin, Denver, Raleigh, and Nashville seeing sustained growth.

This redistribution has had lasting effects on commercial real estate markets, local tax bases, and regional labor pools. It has also allowed some organizations to broaden their talent search: a technology company based in San Francisco can now recruit engineers in Pittsburgh or Boise without expecting them to relocate, expanding the available talent pool significantly.

What Good Hybrid Looks Like

Not all hybrid policies are created equal. Research consistently shows that the most effective hybrid arrangements share several characteristics:

  • Structured coordination: Teams agree on which days to be in the office together, rather than leaving it entirely to individual discretion. Uncoordinated hybrid — where people come in on random days and find themselves alone — delivers few of the benefits of in-person collaboration.
  • Intentional use of in-office time: Effective organizations design in-office days around collaboration, brainstorming, onboarding, and relationship-building — not the heads-down individual work that can be done equally well at home.
  • Equity considerations: Remote work has historically been more available to white-collar workers, and within organizations, access to good remote-work environments varies by income, family situation, and housing type. Companies that ignore these disparities risk deepening internal inequities.

Looking Ahead

The next frontier in the hybrid work debate is management capability. Many managers were promoted based on their ability to manage in-person teams, and leading distributed teams requires a genuinely different skill set — one that emphasizes asynchronous communication, outcome-based evaluation, and intentional relationship-building across digital channels.

Organizations that invest in training managers for the hybrid reality are outperforming those that simply issue policies and assume execution will follow. The physical question of where people work has been largely settled. The human question of how to lead them well remains very much open.
